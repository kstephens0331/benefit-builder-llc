A Section 125 plan only saves money on benefits that the tax code actually allows to be paid pre-tax. One of the most common questions employers ask is a simple one: which benefits qualify? This article walks through the categories that can run through a cafeteria plan, the ones that cannot, and why the distinction matters for your payroll tax savings.

## Insurance premiums, the core of most plans

The most common pre-tax benefit is the employee share of insurance premiums. Health, dental, and vision premiums can almost always be paid through a Section 125 plan, which means the employee pays them before income tax and FICA are calculated. For many employers this single category is the reason to have a plan at all, because premium dollars are predictable and every covered employee uses them.

A plan that covers only premiums has a specific name, a Premium Only Plan, and it is often where a smaller employer starts. We cover that option in detail in [Premium Only Plans explained](/blog/premium-only-plan-pop-explained).

## Flexible spending accounts

A Section 125 plan can also fund flexible spending accounts. A health FSA lets an employee set aside pre-tax dollars for out of pocket medical costs like copays, prescriptions, and other qualified expenses. A dependent care FSA lets an employee set aside pre-tax dollars for child or dependent care so they can work.

FSAs carry their own rules, including annual contribution limits set by the IRS and use it or lose it provisions with limited carryover. Those rules are part of what a properly run plan tracks for you.

## Health savings account contributions

Employees enrolled in a qualified high deductible health plan can contribute to a health savings account, and those contributions can be made pre-tax through a Section 125 plan. This combines two advantages, the pre-tax salary reduction now and the long term tax treatment of the HSA itself. As with FSAs, HSA contributions are subject to annual IRS limits.

## Dependent care assistance

Beyond the dependent care FSA, dependent care assistance is its own qualified category under a cafeteria plan, again within the annual limits the IRS sets. For working parents this is one of the most valued benefits a plan can offer.

## What does not qualify

Just as important as the qualifying list is knowing what cannot run pre-tax through a Section 125 plan. Long term care insurance is generally excluded. Benefits already provided tax free by the employer do not belong in the plan. And certain fringe benefits have their own separate tax rules and are not cafeteria plan benefits.

Trying to push a non-qualifying benefit through the plan is not a savings strategy, it is a compliance risk. Pre-tax treatment that the code does not allow is not defensible if the plan is examined. This is why the qualifying list is not a suggestion, it is the boundary of what the plan can do.

## Why the list matters for your savings

Every qualifying dollar an employee elects is a dollar removed from the wage base for FICA, which is where the employer savings come from. We explain that mechanism in [how FICA savings work](/blog/how-fica-savings-work-for-employers). The more of an employee's real spending that fits a qualifying category, the more both sides save on the same dollars.

That is the practical takeaway. A well designed plan maps the benefits your employees actually use to the categories the code allows, so the savings are maximized and the plan stays compliant.

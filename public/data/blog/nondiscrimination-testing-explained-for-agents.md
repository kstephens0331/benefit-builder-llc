Nondiscrimination testing, usually shortened to NDT, is the part of a Section 125 plan that agents are asked about least and that causes the most year-end stress. The idea behind it is simple: the tax advantages of a cafeteria plan are meant to benefit the whole workforce, not just owners and top earners. The tests confirm that. This guide explains the three core tests in plain terms so you can speak to them with a client.

## Why the tests exist

Section 125 lets employees pay for benefits with pre-tax dollars, which lowers their taxes and the employer's payroll tax. The IRS allows that advantage on the condition that the plan does not disproportionately favor highly compensated employees or key employees, terms the code defines by ownership and pay thresholds. If a plan fails, the favored group can lose the pre-tax treatment and face taxable income, which is exactly the outcome everyone wants to avoid.

## Test 1: Eligibility

The eligibility test looks at who is allowed to participate. A plan cannot set rules that, in practice, only let highly compensated employees in the door. The test examines the classifications used to determine eligibility and whether they unfairly exclude rank-and-file employees. The fix for a borderline plan is usually to broaden eligibility.

## Test 2: Contributions and benefits

The contributions and benefits test looks at what participants actually receive. Even if everyone is technically eligible, a plan can still discriminate if the contributions or benefits skew toward the favored group. This test applies to the plan as a whole, not just the deductions side, so it considers the full picture of who is getting what.

## Test 3: Key employee concentration

The key employee concentration test, often called the 25% test, checks how much of the total pre-tax benefit is going to key employees. If key employees are receiving more than 25% of the total nontaxable benefits provided through the plan, the plan fails this test. This is the one that catches small companies where a few owners or officers make up a large share of the benefit dollars.

## Component tests on top of the core three

Specific benefits carry their own tests. A Dependent Care FSA, for example, has additional nondiscrimination requirements separate from the core cafeteria plan tests. A plan that offers more components has more tests to pass, which is one reason ongoing administration matters.

## How to spot a plan that could fail

A few signs that a client's plan is at risk: a small group where ownership makes up a large share of participation, eligibility rules that quietly exclude part-time or newer staff, or a plan that has never been tested at all. Testing only at the audit is the worst case. Good practice is to test during the plan year so problems can be fixed before the year closes, by broadening eligibility or adjusting participation while there is still time.

## Where this leaves the agent

You do not need to run the tests yourself. That is the administrator's job. What helps you is knowing the three tests exist, what they protect, and that a plan should be tested every year and flagged early if it trends toward failure.
